Topographical & Physical Map of Palestine

Compiled from the Palestine Exploration Fund surveys and other authorities, under the direction of J. G. Bartholomew, and edited by George Adam Smith. Edinburgh, 1901.

The map

This large folding sheet shows the land of Palestine from the Lebanon to the edge of the southern desert, and from the Mediterranean coast across the Jordan into the uplands of Gilead and Moab. It is a physical map first: the shape of the land itself, its plains, ridges, valleys and rifts, is the subject, and the towns, villages, ruins, wells, roads and ancient names are set upon it.

Its most striking feature is the colouring. Bartholomew shows height not by hachures or shading alone but by layer colouring: each band of elevation between contour lines is given its own tint, deepening from the greens of the coastal plain through buffs and browns to the dark umbers of the highest ground. The effect is that the structure of the country can be read at a glance. The long trench of the Jordan valley, falling below the level of the sea to the Dead Sea, stands out in deep green against the brown hill country on either side.

Around the main map are a series of insets and diagrams: a plan of Jerusalem and its environs, a small map of modern Palestine, cross-sections of the country drawn to an exaggerated vertical scale, and a table explaining the Arabic geographical terms that recur in the place names.

John George Bartholomew

John George Bartholomew (1860–1920) was the third generation of an Edinburgh family of map engravers and the most celebrated British cartographer of his age. Under his direction the family firm became the Edinburgh Geographical Institute, producing atlases, wall maps and travel maps whose clarity and colour set a standard across the English-speaking world. He was among the first in Britain to adopt layer colouring systematically, and his reduced-scale survey maps of Britain made it familiar to generations of readers. He was a founder of the Royal Scottish Geographical Society, and was appointed Cartographer to the King. His admirers called him the Prince of Cartographers.

George Adam Smith

George Adam Smith (1856–1942), Scottish minister and Old Testament scholar, published The Historical Geography of the Holy Land in 1894. The book, based on his own travels through the country, argued that the history of Israel and of the early Church could not be understood apart from the land in which it happened: its passes and plains, its watersheds and frontiers. It went through many editions and remains in print. Bartholomew drew the maps for it, and this large sheet is substantially the same map, at a far more generous scale. In 1915 the two men collaborated again on an Atlas of the Historical Geography of the Holy Land.

The survey behind it

The accuracy of the map rests on one of the great field surveys of the nineteenth century. Between 1871 and 1877 the Palestine Exploration Fund sent officers of the Royal Engineers, among them Claude Conder and Herbert Kitchener, to triangulate and map Western Palestine. Working under difficult and sometimes dangerous conditions, they measured base lines, carried a network of triangles across the whole country west of the Jordan, sketched its topography, and recorded the Arabic name of every village, ruin, spring and valley they could find. The result was published in 1880 as twenty-six sheets at one inch to the mile, accompanied by volumes of memoirs and name lists.

The Survey of Western Palestine was the first scientific map of the country, and for decades it was the foundation on which every serious map of the region was built. East of the Jordan, where the Fund’s survey was never completed, Bartholomew drew on the work of later surveyors and travellers. Bartholomew’s achievement was to distil those twenty-six sheets and the other authorities into a single map that could be held in the hands and read as a whole, and to make the relief of the land visible through colour.

This edition

Bartholomew is an attempt to present the map at a resolution that lets you read the smallest lettering on it, on any screen. The image is served as a deep-zoom tile pyramid using the open IIIF image standard, so you can move from the whole sheet down to a single village name without waiting for the full image to load.

The current image is the scan made by Geographicus Rare Antique Maps. Work is under way on a restored master image combining several scans and new photography of the folding map and of the plates in Smith’s Historical Geography, to remove the creases, fraying and discolouration found along the folds of every surviving copy.
